## 1.1 Ceasing Australian tax residency

### The statutory tests

"Resident of Australia" is defined in **s 6(1) ITAA 1936**. Four alternative tests; satisfying
**any one** makes you a resident, so cessation requires failing **all four**:

| Test | Substance | Relevant to this taxpayer? |
|---|---|---|
| **Ordinary concepts ("resides")** | Whether presence in Australia is "usual and settled" rather than "temporary and casual" | **Yes — the primary battleground** |
| **Domicile** | Australian domicile *unless* the Commissioner is satisfied of a **permanent place of abode outside Australia** | **Yes — the second battleground** |
| **183-day** | Present in Australia > 183 days in the income year, unless usual place of abode is outside Australia and no intention to take up residence | Manageable — simply stay away |
| **Commonwealth superannuation** | CSS/PSS members and their families | No |

The Commissioner's consolidated view is **TR 2023/1** (issued 7 June 2023), which replaced
IT 2650, IT 2681 and TR 98/17. Its central message is that there are **no bright-line rules and
no single paramount factor**; the ordinary-concepts test asks whether presence is "usual and
settled in contrast to temporary and casual", informed by the nature, duration and quality of
physical presence *and* an intention to treat Australia as home.

Note also what is **not** law: the Board of Taxation's 2019 recommendation of a primary
**183-day bright-line test** plus a secondary factor test, accepted in the 2021–22 Budget and
consulted on again in 2023, **has still not been legislated as at September 2026**. Planning must
be done against the current, fact-heavy s 6(1) tests. If the bright-line model is ever enacted,
it would make this strategy substantially easier and more certain — that is upside risk, not a
reason to wait.

### The two cases that matter

**Harding v Commissioner of Taxation [2019] FCAFC 29** (special leave refused 13 September 2019)
— the taxpayer win. Harding worked in the Middle East for ~16 years, returned to Queensland in
2006, and left again in 2009 to live in Bahrain while working in Saudi Arabia, in a series of
furnished apartments. The Full Federal Court held his **permanent place of abode was Bahrain**,
establishing that a permanent place of abode need not be a single particular dwelling — it can
be a **country or town**. Gordon J on special leave: "requiring a specific permanent dwelling
seems impractical given the way we currently live."

**Quy and Commissioner of Taxation [2025] ARTA 174** — the taxpayer loss, and the case with this
taxpayer's exact shape. Quy was an Australian citizen, employed by an Australian-connected
multinational, predominantly based in **Dubai** from 2016 to 2020, spending **under two months a
year** in Australia. The Tribunal nonetheless held he was an Australian resident under s 6(1)
throughout. The decisive facts were:

- his **wife and daughters remained continuously in Perth**;
- the **family home remained available to him**, and he stayed there when visiting;
- his presence in Dubai was **solely for an international work assignment** with his
  Australian employer; and
- his ability to remain in Dubai depended entirely on a **temporary residence permit sponsored
  by the host company**.

The result cost him a claimed PAYG refund of **$524,943**. The procedural history matters too:
the first AAT decision ([2024] AATA 245) was quashed by the Federal Court (*Quy v FCT (No 3)*
[2024] FCA 726) and remitted, and the Tribunal reached the **same conclusion** on rehearing.
This is not an outlier that can be argued away.

**The lesson: spending almost no time in Australia does not cease residency if the family, the
home and the economic centre stay behind.**

### Factor-by-factor analysis for this taxpayer

| Factor | Effect if retained | Weight | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spouse in Australia** | Near-fatal (*Quy*) | 🔴 Critical | **Spouse must relocate** |
| **Dependent child in Australia** | Near-fatal | 🔴 Critical | **Child must relocate and enrol in school abroad** |
| **Family home retained and available** | Very strong resident indicator (*Quy*) | 🔴 Critical | Sell, or let on a genuine arm's-length long lease with belongings shipped out |
| **Continuing Australian employment** | Very strong resident indicator (*Quy*) | 🔴 Critical | Resign; take a genuine local-hire foreign contract, not a secondment |
| **Intention to remain overseas** | Central to both tests | 🟠 High | Document contemporaneously; do not paper an intention you do not hold |
| **Permanent home established overseas** | Central to domicile test | 🟠 High | 12-month+ lease or purchase; utilities, school, local bank in own name |
| **Frequency/duration of Australian visits** | Cumulative | 🟠 High | Short holidays only; keep well under 183 days and ideally under ~45 |
| **Australian property (investment)** | Neutral-to-mild | 🟡 Medium | Acceptable — but see §1.7 on the main residence exemption |

### Temporary absence vs genuine cessation

| | Temporary absence | Genuine cessation |
|---|---|---|
| Family | Stays in Australia | Relocates with you |
| Home | Retained and available | Sold or genuinely let out; belongings shipped |
| Employment | Australian employer / secondment | Resignation; local-hire foreign contract |
| Foreign accommodation | Company-provided, short-term, sponsor-tied | Own long lease or purchase in own name |
| Duration/intention | Fixed-term, return date known | Open-ended; no return date |
| Return visits | Frequent, extended, "home" | Occasional, short, as a visitor |
| Foreign integration | Minimal | School, bank, licence, health cover, community |
| Outcome | **Still an Australian resident** (*Quy*) | **Non-resident** (*Harding*) |

### Documentation to retain (contemporaneous, not reconstructed)

Departure date evidence (flight records, passport stamps); foreign lease or title; foreign
utility accounts; foreign employment contract; school enrolment for the child; foreign bank
account opening documents; foreign tax residency certificate; removalist/shipping invoices;
Australian lease or sale contract for the home; employment termination letter; Medicare
Entitlement Statement; cancelled Australian memberships and subscriptions; ATO overseas travel
notification (mandatory if you hold a HELP debt and will be overseas 183+ days); and a
contemporaneous written record of intention. The ATO's own guidance stresses that
**contemporaneous evidence is far more persuasive than after-the-fact explanation**.

## 1.2 CGT event I1 on departure

### The provision

**ITAA 1997 s 104‑160** — *Individual or company stops being an Australian resident: CGT event I1*.

- **Trigger:** you stop being an Australian resident. **Time of event:** when you stop.
- **Scope:** you work out a capital gain or loss for **each CGT asset you owned just before the
  event, except one that is taxable Australian property**.
- **Mechanics:** deemed disposal at **market value** at the cessation time.

### Does Bitcoin trigger I1? Yes.

Bitcoin is a **CGT asset** under s 108‑5 and is **not** within any of the five categories of
taxable Australian property in the s 855‑15 table (see §1.4). It is therefore squarely caught.
The ATO says so directly in its guidance page *Crypto asset transactions and tax residency*,
which works an example: "Emma… spends $10,000 to buy Bitcoin… Emma makes a capital gain of
$12,000 ($22,000 market value − $10,000 cost base) in the income year she stops being a
resident. Emma has the option of disregarding the capital gain at that time."

Note the corollary, which Pitcher Partners puts crisply: **I1 is triggered by a change of
residency, not by asset location — moving coins to a foreign exchange or a foreign wallet
achieves nothing.**

### The escape hatches, and why all of them are closed

There are exactly three statutory routes out of CGT event I1. It is worth naming them, because
two are real provisions that genuinely exempt the gain — not merely defer it — and a taxpayer
looking for "the obscure method" will eventually find them. Both are closed to this taxpayer.

**1. The short-term resident exception — former s 104‑165(1).** Until 2006 an individual could
**disregard** the I1 gain outright if they had been an Australian resident for **fewer than 5 of
the previous 10 years** and either owned the asset before last becoming a resident or acquired it
through a death. This was a true exemption, not a deferral. It was **repealed** by the *Tax Laws
Amendment (2006 Measures No. 4) Act 2006*, Sch 1 item 20, for CGT events happening on or after
**1 July 2006**.

It survives only through **s 104‑166 of the *Income Tax (Transitional Provisions) Act 1997***,
which preserves it for a person who was in Australia on **6 April 2006**, remained an Australian
resident continuously from that day until ceasing, and was resident for fewer than 5 of the 10
years before ceasing. **A lifelong Australian citizen fails this comprehensively** — and on
6 April 2006 a taxpayer who is 34 in 2026 was 14 years old. 🔴 Closed.

**2. The temporary resident exemption — s 768‑915.** An individual who was a **temporary
resident** immediately before ceasing Australian residency disregards the I1 gain entirely. But
"temporary resident" is defined in **s 995‑1(1)** as a person who holds a temporary visa under the
*Migration Act 1958*, is **not** an Australian resident within the meaning of the *Social Security
Act 1991*, and has no spouse who is. Australian citizens **are** Australian residents under that
Act. **An Australian citizen can therefore never be a temporary resident.** 🔴 Categorically closed.

**3. The carve-out inside s 104‑160 itself.** The section excludes assets that are taxable
Australian property under **items 1, 3 or 4** of the s 855‑15 table — Australian real property,
assets used through an Australian permanent establishment, and options over them. Bitcoin is none
of these. 🔴 Not available.

> **The conclusion this forces:** the exit charge is the one genuinely unavoidable element of the
> strategy, and it is unavoidable **by design**. Parliament closed the last general exemption in
> 2006 precisely because departing residents were using it this way. Anyone offering an "obscure
> method" to escape CGT event I1 on these facts is describing either a repealed provision, a
> provision that requires not being an Australian citizen, or non-disclosure.

### The calculation on these facts

| | |
|---|---|
| Market value at cessation | $1,200,000 |
| Cost base | $200,000 |
| Gross capital gain (I1) | **$1,000,000** |
| Held > 12 months? | Yes |
| 50% CGT discount available? | **Yes — see below** |
| Net capital gain | **$500,000** |

**Why the full 50% discount applies.** Subdiv 115‑B (ss 115‑105, 115‑115) reduces the discount
percentage by reference to days in the **discount testing period** — which runs from acquisition
of the asset to the CGT event — on which you were a foreign or temporary resident. Because the
I1 event happens at the *moment* residency ceases, the taxpayer was an Australian resident for
**every day** of the testing period. Discount percentage = 50%. This is a significant and
under-appreciated advantage of crystallising: **the I1 event is the last moment at which the
full 50% discount is available on this asset.**

### Market value: evidence

The ATO requires the market value on the exact cessation date, from a "reliable source". For a
$1.2m Bitcoin position, keep: timestamped exchange screenshots from two or more major venues;
the RBA or ATO daily AUD/USD rate; a written valuation methodology (e.g. volume-weighted average
of nominated exchanges at a nominated UTC time); and, for a position of this size, consider a
formal valuation report. Retain wallet balances and on-chain confirmations at the same
timestamp.

### Timing: the single most valuable legitimate lever

The I1 gain falls into the income year in which residency ceases and is taxed at **resident
marginal rates**, stacked on that year's other assessable income. Ceasing residency in the
**first days of an Australian income year** (i.e. early July), after employment has ended in the
prior income year, means the $500,000 net capital gain is taxed almost alone:

| Departure timing | Other Australian income that year | Modelled tax on the I1 gain |
|---|---|---|
| Late in income year (e.g. May) | ~$150,000 salary | **$231,800** |
| Early in income year (e.g. 10 July), employment ended 30 June | ~$0 | **≈$192,400** |

**≈$39,000 saved by choosing the departure date.** This is ordinary timing, not avoidance: the
law taxes the gain in the year the event happens, and the taxpayer chooses when to leave. Note
that a part-year resident gets a reduced tax-free threshold of **$13,464 + $395 per month of
residency** (so $13,859 for one month), and the Medicare levy is pro-rated for the
foreign-resident portion of the year. Watch for termination payments, accrued leave payouts and
bonuses landing in the departure year — plan for them to fall in the prior year.

## 1.3 The central question: crystallise I1, or elect to disregard it?

### The election — ITAA 1997 s 104‑165

- **s 104‑165(2):** an individual **may choose** to disregard making a capital gain or capital
  loss from **all** CGT assets covered by CGT event I1.
- **s 104‑165(3):** if you so choose, each of those assets is **taken to be taxable Australian
  property** until the **earlier** of (a) a CGT event happening in relation to the asset that
  involves you ceasing to own it, and (b) you **again becoming an Australian resident**.

Three features of the choice are decisive:

1. **All-or-nothing.** It applies to every asset caught by I1. You cannot crystallise one parcel
   and defer another.
2. **Irrevocable.** Made by lodging the income tax return for the year (the general choice rule
   in s 103‑25); it cannot be unwound later.
3. **It is deferral, not exemption.** The whole architecture preserves Australia's taxing rights.

### Scenario A — allow I1 to crystallise

| | |
|---|---|
| Australian tax at departure (optimised timing) | **≈$192,400** |
| New cost base of the Bitcoin | **$1,200,000** (deemed disposal and reacquisition) |
| Status of the Bitcoin afterwards | Ordinary CGT asset, **not TAP** |
| Sale while a foreign resident (year 7, $3m) | **$0 Australian tax** — s 855‑10 disregards it |
| Return to Australia still holding | s 855‑45 resets cost base to market value on re-entry |
| **Total lifetime Australian tax** | **≈$192,400** |

### Scenario B — elect to disregard

| | |
|---|---|
| Australian tax at departure | $0 |
| Cost base of the Bitcoin | **unchanged at $200,000** |
| Status of the Bitcoin afterwards | **Deemed taxable Australian property** |
| Australia retains taxing rights? | **Yes — over the entire gain, from the original cost base** |
| Australia taxes the pre-departure gain? | **Yes** |
| Australia taxes post-departure appreciation? | **Yes — all of it** |
| CGT discount still relevant? | Yes, but **apportioned down** under s 115‑115 |
| Sale while a foreign resident (year 7, $3m) | **≈$944,600 Australian tax** |
| **Total lifetime Australian tax** | **≈$944,600** |

**Working for Scenario B** (acquisition 6 years before departure, sale 7 years after):

```
Gross gain                 $3,000,000 − $200,000        = $2,800,000
Discount testing period    13 years; resident 6 of 13
Discount percentage        50% × (6/13)                 = 23.08%   (s 115-115)
Taxable capital gain       $2,800,000 × 76.92%          = $2,153,850
Foreign-resident tax       $135,000 @ 30%               =    $40,500
                           $55,000 @ 37%                =    $20,350
                           $1,963,850 @ 45%             =   $883,733
Medicare levy              nil (foreign residents exempt)
TOTAL                                                   ≈  $944,600
```

Two compounding penalties drive that number: the discount collapses from 50% to ~23%, and
foreign-resident rates start at **30% from the first dollar with no tax-free threshold**.

### Does the timing of the sale change Scenario B? Yes — it gets worse over time

| Sale timing after departure | Discount % | Taxable gain (sale at $3m) | Australian tax |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 year | 50% × 6/7 = 42.9% | $1,600,000 | ≈$695,000 |
| 2 years | 50% × 6/8 = 37.5% | $1,750,000 | ≈$762,900 |
| 5 years | 50% × 6/11 = 27.3% | $2,036,400 | ≈$891,700 |
| 10 years | 50% × 6/16 = 18.8% | $2,275,000 | ≈$999,100 |

The longer you hold under the election, the **smaller** the discount and the larger the
Australian liability. The election penalises exactly the patient, long-term holder it appears
to help.

### The verdict

> **Do not make the s 104‑165 choice.** 🟢

Crystallising costs **$192,400**. Electing costs **$944,600** — a **$752,200** difference — and,
as §4 shows, the election is worse than **never leaving Australia at all** in every price
scenario modelled.

The only circumstance in which the election is rational is if you are confident a tax treaty will
strip Australia of the taxing right it just preserved (see §3.4). That trade is: **maximum upside
$192,400; downside $752,200.** You would need roughly **80% confidence** in an uncertain,
MLI-principal-purpose-tested treaty argument merely to break even. That is not a trade a
competent adviser signs off on.

**When the election *is* sensible** — and this is worth stating so the recommendation is not
mistaken for a general rule — is the illiquid case: a departing taxpayer whose I1 assets are
unlisted shares or an interest in a private business that cannot be sold to fund the tax. That
is not this taxpayer. Bitcoin is liquid.

### ⚠️ The most common misconception: "there is no treaty with the UAE, so Australia cannot tax it"

This is the single most expensive error available in this plan, and it is intuitive enough that
it needs stating explicitly.

**A tax treaty never creates a taxing right. It only restricts rights that domestic law has
already conferred.** Applied here:

| Step | Effect |
|---|---|
| s 104‑165(3) deems the Bitcoin to be TAP | Australia's charge arises under **domestic law** |
| s 855‑10 disregards a foreign resident's gain only on a **non**-TAP asset | Switched off by the deeming |
| A DTA *could* override that (Agreements Act s 4(2)) if its alienation article gave exclusive residence-state rights | **Only a treaty can remove the charge** |
| **No treaty exists with the UAE** | **Nothing removes the charge. It stands in full.** |

So the absence of an Australia–UAE DTA does not free the gain — it **guarantees** Australia keeps
it. The UAE is not the jurisdiction where the election works; it is the one place where the
election can *never* work. Selling in a 0% jurisdiction changes only the *destination's* tax; it
has no effect whatever on Australia's deemed-TAP charge.

**The related "no collection assistance" idea fails for the same fact pattern.** It is true that
Australia has no treaty mechanism to enforce a debt against UAE assets. But that concerns
enforcement, not liability: the obligation to lodge and declare persists, the debt accrues general
interest charge at roughly 11% compounding (≈$2.18m on $944,600 after eight years), and every
enforcement difficulty evaporates the moment the taxpayer returns to Australia — which is the
express plan. Relying on it is not deferral; it is non-disclosure.

**Note what this means for the recommended route.** By crystallising I1, the Bitcoin becomes an
ordinary non-TAP asset and s 855‑10 disregards the later gain under **domestic law alone**. No
treaty is required, nothing needs to be argued, and there is nothing to conceal — the gain is
genuinely not taxable and the position survives full disclosure. That is precisely why the UAE's
lack of a treaty costs nothing on the recommended path, while being fatal on the aggressive one.

## 1.4 Selling Bitcoin as a foreign resident

### The TAP definition

**ITAA 1997 s 855‑10** disregards a capital gain or loss made by a foreign resident from a CGT
event **unless** the asset is **taxable Australian property**. **s 855‑15** sets out the
exhaustive table of five categories:

| # | Category |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Taxable Australian real property** (TARP) — Australian land, buildings, mining rights |
| 2 | **Indirect Australian real property interests** — membership interests passing the non-portfolio and principal asset tests |
| 3 | Assets used at any time in carrying on a business **through an Australian permanent establishment** |
| 4 | **Options or rights** to acquire a category 1, 2 or 3 asset |
| 5 | Assets **deemed to be TAP** by the s 104‑165(3) choice (via the Income Tax (Transitional Provisions) Act 1997) |

**Bitcoin held personally as an investment falls into none of categories 1–4.** It is not land,
not an interest in a land-rich entity, not used in an Australian business, and not an option.
It is not TAP merely because its owner used to be an Australian resident, or because it was
acquired while resident, or because it sits on an Australian exchange.

### The two outcomes

| | **No I1 election** | **I1 gain disregarded** |
|---|---|---|
| Is the Bitcoin TAP? | **No** | **Yes — category 5, by deeming** |
| Does s 855‑10 disregard the gain? | **Yes** | **No** |
| Australian tax on sale as a foreign resident | **$0** | Full gain from original cost base, discount apportioned |
| Does it matter whether the sale is 1, 5 or 10 years later? | No — always $0 | Yes — **worse the longer you wait** |

### Crypto-specific confirmation

This is not an inference from the share-CGT rules. The ATO's crypto guidance addresses it
directly: "**A crypto asset may become taxable Australian property (TAP) where you stop being an
Australian resident and choose to disregard a capital gain or capital loss made under CGT event
I1.**" The clear implication — that absent the choice a crypto asset is *not* TAP — matches the
s 855‑15 table.

**Certainty: 🟢 High.** The statutory route is clean and the ATO's crypto-specific guidance is
on point.

## 1.5 Returning to Australia in 5–10 years

### The re-entry provision

**ITAA 1997 s 855‑45** — *Individual or company becomes an Australian resident*. Where you
become an Australian resident and owned a CGT asset just before doing so, and the asset was
**not** taxable Australian property just before that time (and was not a pre‑20 September 1985
asset):

- the **first element of cost base and reduced cost base is the asset's market value** at the
  time you become a resident; and
- **"Parts 3‑1 and 3‑3 apply to the asset as if you had acquired it at the time you became an
  Australian resident."**

That second limb matters as much as the first: it **restarts the 12-month clock and the discount
testing period**, so a full 50% discount is available on a post-return sale after 12 months of
holding, uncontaminated by the foreign-resident years.

### Walking the brief's timeline

| Year | Event | Australian tax consequence (no I1 election) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Resident. BTC $1.2m, cost base $200k | — |
| 1 | **Cease residency** | CGT event I1: $1m gain, $500k net, **≈$192,400 tax**. Cost base resets to $1.2m |
| 2 | BTC $1.5m | **Nil.** Foreign resident, non-TAP, no CGT event |
| 5 | BTC $3m | **Nil** |
| 7 | **Sell all BTC for $3m** as a resident of a 0% jurisdiction | **Nil** — s 855‑10 disregards the $1.8m gain |
| 8 | **Return to Australia** | **Nil.** No retrospective liability |

### Can Australia tax any of it on return?

| Amount | Can Australia tax it on return? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Original $1m gain | **No** | Already taxed at I1 in year 1 |
| $1.8m post-departure gain | **No** | CGT event happened while a foreign resident; asset not TAP; s 855‑10 |
| Any gain realised while non-resident | **No** | Same |
| Appreciation after returning | **Yes** | Ordinary resident CGT, from the s 855‑45 market-value base |
| Proceeds sitting in an offshore bank account | **No** on the corpus | Capital, not income. Interest **accruing after** residency resumes is assessable worldwide income |
| Replacement investments bought with proceeds | Only post-return growth | s 855‑45 resets their cost base to market value on re-entry |
| Cash brought back to Australia | **No** | Remittance of capital is not a taxable event. Australia has no remittance basis |

**There is no retrospective CGT on becoming an Australian resident again.** Australia's re-entry
rule is a cost-base *step-up*, not a clawback.

### Does it matter whether you sell before or after returning?

Less than most people assume — but selling **before** is cleaner.

- **Sell before returning:** gain disregarded under s 855‑10. Outcome locked in. Nothing depends
  on a later valuation.
- **Return still holding, sell later:** s 855‑45 resets the cost base to market value on the day
  residency resumes, so the pre-return gain **still escapes**. But you carry (i) market risk
  through the transition, (ii) a valuation and evidence burden on the re-entry-day market value,
  (iii) a fresh 12-month wait for the discount, and (iv) exposure to any change of law between
  now and then.

**Recommendation: sell before returning.** 🟢

### The election trap on return — ATO ID 2009/148

If the s 104‑165 choice **was** made and you return still holding the asset, the deemed-TAP
status ends *at* the moment you resume residency (s 104‑165(3)(b)). But s 855‑45 requires the
asset to have been **not TAP immediately before** you became a resident — and it was. **ATO ID
2009/148** confirms the consequence: where the property had the necessary connection with
Australia because of the earlier s 104‑165(2) choice, **s 855‑45 does not apply, so there is no
deemed acquisition and the cost base is not reset**. The original $200,000 cost base survives,
and Australia taxes the entire accumulated gain on eventual sale.

**This is the worst outcome in the entire analysis, and it is reached by choosing what looks
like the tax-deferral option.** 🔴

## 1.6 Crypto transaction mechanics: what does and does not trigger CGT

Distinguishing **transfer without disposal** from **exchange/disposal** is the difference between
a nil event and a $470,000 tax bill.

| Action | CGT event in Australia? | Authority / note |
|---|---|---|
| Moving BTC between wallets **you** control | **No** | ATO: moving crypto "will generally not be considered a transfer of ownership if you remain as the owner". Beneficial ownership is the test |
| Moving BTC from an exchange to self-custody | **No** | Same — but the **network fee paid in BTC is itself a disposal** of that fee amount |
| Changing custodians (you remain beneficial owner) | **No** | Same |
| Moving BTC to an offshore exchange | **No** | And it does **not** avoid I1 — I1 follows residency, not asset location |
| Multisig arrangements where you retain beneficial ownership | **No** | Fact-dependent; document the key structure |
| **Crypto-to-crypto swap** (BTC → ETH) | **Yes — CGT event A1** | Each swap is a disposal at market value |
| **BTC → stablecoin** | **Yes — A1** | A stablecoin is a different CGT asset |
| **Wrapping / unwrapping BTC (wBTC)** | **Yes** | ATO draft **TD 2026/D2** (19 August 2026): wrapping triggers a CGT event and unwrapping triggers another. Capital proceeds = market value of the wrapped token |
| **Converting BTC into a spot BTC ETF** | **Yes — A1** | An ETF unit is a different asset |
| **Derivatives** (futures, options over BTC) | Separate CGT assets | Entering a derivative is not a disposal of the underlying, but the derivative has its own CGT profile; can also be revenue account |
| **DeFi "lending"** where coins go to an address you don't control | **Usually yes** | ATO: beneficial ownership ends; A1, C2, E2 or H2 |
| **Gifting BTC** (including to a spouse) | **Yes — A1 at market value** | s 116‑30 market value substitution |
| **Genuine secured loan** (BTC pledged as security) | **No** | **s 106‑60** — see §1.9 |

**Practical rule for the 12 months before departure: do nothing to the Bitcoin except hold it and
move it between wallets you control.** Every clever manoeuvre in the list above is a realisation
event at full resident rates.

## 1.7 The Australian home — a trap that dwarfs most of the crypto planning

Since the *Treasury Laws Amendment (Reducing Pressure on Housing Affordability Measures) Act
2019* (law from December 2019), a **foreign resident at the date of a CGT event on a dwelling
loses the main residence exemption entirely** — not pro-rata, but in full, back to the date of
acquisition.

- Properties acquired **after 9 May 2017**: rule applies immediately.
- Properties owned at 9 May 2017: transitional relief expired **30 June 2020**.
- **Life events exception:** available only if you were a foreign resident for a **continuous
  period of 6 years or less** *and* a specified life event occurred (terminal illness, death of
  a spouse/child, or a CGT event arising from a formal agreement on relationship breakdown).
  Ordinary emigration is **not** a life event.

**Implications for this taxpayer:**

- If the family home has significant embedded gain, **sell it while still an Australian
  resident**, before residency ceases. Selling it as a foreign resident forfeits the entire
  exemption.
- Alternatively, keep it and **sell only after resuming Australian residency** — the exemption is
  tested at the date of the CGT event, so a resident vendor is fine, though the absence period
  will interact with the s 118‑145 "absence rule" (six years if income-producing, indefinite if
  not).
- Also note **foreign resident capital gains withholding**: 15% of the sale price on Australian
  real property from 1 January 2025, with no value threshold, unless a clearance certificate is
  obtained.
- **Land tax surcharges:** NSW (5%), Victoria (4% absentee owner) and Queensland (3%) surcharges
  **do not apply to Australian citizens** living overseas. This is one thing citizenship does
  buy.

## 1.8 Parcel selection

Australia permits **specific identification** of crypto parcels on disposal, provided records
support it — there is no statutory FIFO rule for crypto. The taxpayer chooses which parcel is
disposed of, and the choice drives the cost base and the 12-month holding test.

**But note a structural point specific to this strategy: parcel selection is irrelevant to CGT
event I1.** I1 deems a disposal of **every** affected asset simultaneously, so all parcels are
disposed of at once and the aggregate gain is the same however parcels are labelled. Parcel
selection matters only for **partial disposals before departure** — for example, selling a
tranche to fund the departure tax bill.

| Parcel selected for a pre-departure partial sale | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Highest cost base** | Smallest current gain — but the remaining low-base parcels are then caught by I1 anyway, so this only shifts *which year*, not *how much* |
| **Lowest cost base** | Largest current gain |
| **Oldest** | Guarantees the >12 month discount |
| **Newest** | Risks failing the 12-month test — **avoid any parcel held < 12 months** |

**Evidentiary requirements:** date and time of each acquisition; quantity; AUD value at
acquisition (with the exchange rate source); the wallet/exchange it sits in; transaction IDs;
fees; and an auditable, consistently applied identification method. The ATO's crypto data
matching program collects records on 700,000–1.2 million individuals per year covering every
year from 2014–15 to 2025–26 — inconsistent parcel records will be noticed.

**Key insight:** because I1 catches everything, the only parcels worth selling before departure
are those needed to fund the tax bill, and they should be **the oldest, highest-cost-base
parcels held more than 12 months**.

## 1.9 Capital loss harvesting

### The ordering rule

The s 102‑5 method statement applies capital losses **against the gross capital gain, before the
50% discount**. The consequence is arithmetically important and widely misunderstood:

> **Against a discountable gain, $1 of capital loss saves only about 22.6 cents of tax, not 47
> cents.** The loss is effectively halved.

If the taxpayer has both discountable and non-discountable gains, losses should be applied to
the **non-discounted** gains first — the method statement allows the taxpayer to choose the
order in which discount gains are reduced.

### Model (optimised early-July departure, $1m gross I1 gain)

| Capital losses available | Net gain after losses | Net capital gain (after 50%) | Modelled Australian tax | Tax saved | Value per $1 of loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0 | $1,000,000 | $500,000 | $192,400 | — | — |
| $50,000 | $950,000 | $475,000 | $181,100 | $11,300 | 22.6c |
| $100,000 | $900,000 | $450,000 | $169,800 | $22,600 | 22.6c |
| $200,000 | $800,000 | $400,000 | $147,200 | $45,200 | 22.6c |
| $500,000 | $500,000 | $250,000 | $79,450 | $112,950 | 22.6c |

Carried-forward capital losses from prior years are equally usable and are applied oldest-first
after current-year losses.

**No wash sales.** Selling an asset at a loss and reacquiring substantially the same asset with
no change in economic exposure is squarely within the ATO's wash sale guidance (TA 2008/7) and
Part IVA. Genuine disposals of genuinely impaired assets only.

## 1.10 BTC-backed loans — liquidity without a disposal

### The Australian CGT position

**ITAA 1997 s 106‑60** (*Securities, charges and encumbrances*): where a CGT asset of a borrower
vests in a lender as **security for a loan**, no CGT event happens on the vesting; and if the
lender later sells the asset under the security arrangement, the **capital gain or loss is made
by the borrower**, not the lender. This is the provision that makes genuine BTC-backed lending
CGT-neutral.

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Are loan proceeds assessable income? | **No.** Borrowing is not income |
| Is pledging BTC as security a CGT event? | **No** — s 106‑60, provided it is a genuine security arrangement |
| Does moving BTC to a custodian trigger CGT? | **Depends on beneficial ownership.** If the custodian can rehypothecate or commingle, beneficial ownership may end → CGT event A1. Segregated, non-rehypothecated custody with the borrower as beneficial owner should be safe. A successful ATO private ruling on the Australian lender **Vield** confirmed its product did not trigger CGT |
| Does lender liquidation on a margin call trigger CGT? | **Yes — for the borrower** (s 106‑60(3)). A forced liquidation while still an Australian resident realises the gain at full resident rates. **This is the key risk** |
| Is loan interest deductible? | Only if the borrowed funds are used to produce **assessable** income. Funding relocation and living costs is **not** deductible. Once non-resident with no Australian assessable income, deductibility is moot |
| Can a loan fund the relocation and the I1 tax bill? | **Yes — and this is its best use here** |

### Comparison of routes

| Route | CGT-neutral? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Conventional secured BTC loan** (Ledn, Unchained, Arch) | 🟢 Yes | Ledn: ~50% max LTV, ~9.25–11.49% APR, BitGo Trust cold storage, ring-fenced. Unchained: 2-of-3 multisig, borrower holds a key — the strongest beneficial-ownership position. Arch: up to 60% LTV, Anchorage custody, no rehypothecation |
| **Institutional crypto lending** | 🟢 Usually | Check the terms for rehypothecation rights — those are what break s 106‑60 |
| **Offshore lending** | 🟢 Usually | Same analysis; adds counterparty and jurisdiction risk |
| **DeFi lending** | 🔴 **No** | ATO: beneficial ownership generally ends when tokens go to a pool address you don't control → CGT event |
| **Borrowing through a company** | 🔴 | Getting the BTC into the company is itself a market-value disposal, and companies get no 50% discount |
| **Borrowing personally** | 🟢 | The right answer |

### Strategic use — and one important warning

A modest, low-LTV BTC-backed loan is a sensible way to fund relocation costs and the ≈$192,400
I1 liability **without** selling coins. Keep LTV very conservative (≤25%) so a drawdown cannot
force liquidation.

**Warning — Switzerland.** If Switzerland is the destination, a BTC-backed loan may be
counter-productive: one of the five cumulative safe-harbour criteria in Swiss Federal Tax
Administration **Circular No. 36 (27 July 2012)** for *private wealth management* is **no use of
borrowed capital**. Failing it risks reclassification as a professional securities dealer, which
turns tax-free capital gains into taxable self-employment income.

## 1.11 Spouse, family and entity structures

**Question:** can any structure legally reduce Australian tax on the **existing $1m unrealised
gain**? **Answer: essentially no.** Every route requires transferring the Bitcoin, and every
transfer is a disposal at market value.

| Structure | Effect on the existing gain | Deferral or permanent reduction? |
|---|---|---|
| **Transfer to spouse** | CGT event A1 **now** at market value (s 116‑30 market value substitution — non-arm's-length / no consideration). Triggers the whole $1m gain immediately | **Neither — it accelerates the tax** |
| **Joint ownership** | Same: creating a half-interest is a part-disposal at market value | Acceleration |
| **Marriage breakdown rollover** (Subdiv 126‑A) | Available only on genuine relationship breakdown. Not available to an intact marriage, and contriving one would be a sham | N/A |
| **Discretionary / family trust** | CGT event E2 or A1 at market value on settling the asset. Trust income later taxed to beneficiaries; no additional discount benefit | Acceleration, then modest income-splitting on **future** income only |
| **Company** | A1 at market value on transfer, **and companies get no 50% CGT discount** — 25–30% flat on the full gain, with a second layer of tax on distribution | **Strictly worse** |
| **SMSF** | **Legally impossible.** SIS Act **s 66** prohibits an SMSF acquiring an asset from a related party; crypto is not within the listed-security or business-real-property exceptions. An in-specie contribution of Bitcoin cannot be made | N/A |
| **Superannuation (cash contributions)** | Personal deductible contributions reduce taxable income in the departure year. 2026–27 concessional cap **$32,500**, plus carry-forward of up to ~**$175,000** if total super balance < $500,000 at the prior 30 June | **Permanent rate reduction** — but see below |
| **Family partnership** | Same disposal analysis as joint ownership | Acceleration |

### The one structure that does work: concessional superannuation contributions

Making a large personal deductible contribution in the departure year converts income taxed at
**47%** into contributions taxed at **30%** (15% contributions tax + 15% Division 293, since
income will exceed the $250,000 threshold).

```
Carry-forward + current concessional cap (2026-27)   ≈ $175,000
Tax saved at 47% marginal                            ≈  $82,250
Less contributions tax + Div 293 at 30%              ≈ ($52,500)
NET PERMANENT SAVING                                 ≈  $29,750
```

**Trade-offs to weigh honestly:** the money is preserved until age 60 — **26 years** for this
taxpayer; the taxpayer must have $175,000 of cash to contribute; and superannuation is subject to
material legislative risk over that horizon. A ~$30,000 saving for a 26-year lock-up is a
genuine but not compelling trade. 🟡

**Deferral vs permanent reduction — the distinction the brief asks for:**

- **Permanent reduction:** superannuation contributions (rate arbitrage); genuine capital losses;
  departure-year timing; the 50% discount itself.
- **Deferral only:** the s 104‑165 choice (and, as shown, deferral at a *higher* eventual rate).
- **Acceleration (i.e. actively harmful):** spouse transfers, trusts, companies, partnerships.

## 1.12 Information reporting, CRS, CARF and AML

This is not an evasion exercise, so the working assumption is **full visibility**. What Australian
authorities will see:

| Channel | Status |
|---|---|
| **OECD Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF)** | Australia announced implementation at MYEFO on 17 December 2025. Reporting **from 2027**, first international exchanges **2028**. Foreign exchanges in participating jurisdictions will report Australian-resident account holders to their own authority, which passes it to the ATO |
| **Domestic crypto reporting regime** | Separate Australian regime announced alongside CARF; commences 2027, reporting to the ATO from 2028 |
| **Common Reporting Standard** | Operating since 2018 across 100+ jurisdictions. Bank balances and income reported. **The UAE participates in CRS** |
| **ATO crypto data matching** | 700,000–1.2 million individuals/entities per year, covering 2014–15 to 2025–26. Collects names, addresses, DOB, phone, email, social media, bank details, wallet addresses, transaction dates/times/types/quantities |
| **AUSTRAC** | Australian digital currency exchange providers are registered designated service providers, compelled to produce data |
| **Banking records** | International funds transfer instructions reported to AUSTRAC; the ATO receives them |

**Consequence for planning:** the ATO will be able to see that (i) the taxpayer held Bitcoin
while resident, (ii) the coins moved offshore, and (iii) a large disposal occurred. The strategy
must therefore be *correct*, not merely *undetected*. It is designed to be.

### Records to retain — a permanent file

**Ownership and cost base**
- Every acquisition: date, time, quantity, AUD price, exchange rate source, exchange, transaction ID
- Wallet addresses and the full chain of custody from acquisition to disposal
- Exchange statements and, where self-custodied, signed messages proving control

**Cessation of Australian residency**
- Departure date evidence; foreign lease/title; school enrolment; foreign employment contract
- Australian home sale or lease; employment termination letter; shipping invoices
- Medicare Entitlement Statement; ATO overseas travel notification
- Final Australian return showing the I1 gain declared

**Foreign residency**
- Foreign **tax residency certificate for each year** (this is the single most important document)
- Residence visa; entry/exit stamps; a day-count log
- Foreign tax returns; evidence of foreign tax paid (or of the legal basis for nil tax)

**Disposal**
- Sale date, venue, counterparty, proceeds, AUD equivalent, exchange rate source
- Contemporaneous evidence that residency abroad was in place at the sale date

**Return to Australia**
- Date of resumption; market value of every asset held on that date (for the s 855‑45 reset)
- Bank statements showing the source and path of remitted funds

Retain everything for at least **five years after the last relevant return**, and realistically
for the life of the assets.
